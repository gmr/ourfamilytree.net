Kent and Kint Records Map

Land, tax, census, military, court and vital records for the Kent, Kint and Kindt families of the Northern New Jersey and Southern New York corridor, 1720 to 1890.

Records were transcribed with AI assistance and may contain errors. Check each against the original. Names are as written, and one name may be several people; nothing here asserts kinship. Map positions are approximate, not to survey.

How to use this map
  • Choose what to see. In Filters, pick counties or townships and tick the kinds of record. The map and every list follow that choice.
  • Read the markers. Numbered circle = a township as the records name it; the number is how many records name it; its pop-up window lists them in order of year, each with a coloured badge for its kind of record (Land, Tax, Census, Military, Court, B/M/D, Misc). Numbered squares in the bar directly below the map = records that name only the county (they are not placed on the map). Letter = one particular place in Morris County. ◇ = the Swine Field court case. Hover over a marker for a summary; click it for a pop-up window listing its records.
  • Look at one township. Click a township name on the map, or pick one from the "Jump to place" list in Filters, under Years (◀ and ▶ step to the previous or next place). The map zooms to it straight away and its records open in a panel beside the map. You can also pick a whole county from the same list. Choose "Currently Entire Map" (or press "× Entire map" on the panel) to close the panel and return to the full-width map.
  • Find a record. Under the map, each township lists all its records in order of year. A coloured badge in the Type column shows the kind: land, tax ratables and lists, census, military, court, birth, marriage and death, or miscellaneous. Tick or untick the kinds in "Record types" (in Filters), and use "Order records" to switch between "By year" and the older "By record type" tabs. The Index of individuals (see the links at the top of the page) opens with resolved individuals and family groups, then lists every name with the records that name it.
  • Terms. "Township only" = the record names an old township, not a lot, so no exact spot is shown. "n.d." = no date recorded. "Deed workbook" = the compiler’s deed research workbook; a dash in a book-and-page column = book and page were not recorded. † = acreage taken from the compiler’s research notes, not read from the deed.

        Understanding Individual and Family Groups

        The records on this page are raw material: names exactly as a clerk wrote them. An individual or a family group is a conclusion built on top of them: “these records are about the same man”, or “these people were a household”. The page keeps the two apart. Choosing a person or group in Filters & quick jump never changes a record; it only narrows the list to the records that have been linked to that choice and ringed on the map.

        • Proven means a record states the connection directly (for example a deed that names the person and the relationship). Probable means the evidence is indirect but points the same way. Every link also carries a one-sentence explanation, shown in the pop-up.
        • Rings. A solid ring at a location means at least one Proven record for the selection was made there; a dashed ring means Probable records only. The badge is the number of linked records at that spot. The ring shows where a record was written about, not necessarily where the person lived.
        • One name, several men. Kent, Kint and Kindt families repeat given names (several Jacobs, Eliases and Johns). A shared name alone never links a record to a person here.
        Methodology (how links are made)
        • Links follow the Genealogical Proof Standard: a reasonably thorough search, a citation for each record, analysis of what each record can and cannot show, resolution of conflicting evidence, and a written conclusion. Friends, associates and neighbours (the FAN club) are used to separate men of the same name.
        • A family group lists each member with the documented relationship, its own confidence level and a supporting citation. Relationships are entered by the researcher; nothing is inferred from names, dates or neighbours.
        • An individual may belong to more than one family group (for example the family he was born into and the one he married into).
        • A record’s place on the map is the township or lettered place the page already uses for it. County-only records have no marker, so they are counted in the summary but not ringed.
        • Research is kept on the server by the project administrators. Visitors see a profile or family group once an administrator publishes it.

        East Jersey and West Jersey dividing lines

        None of the lines was surveyed to modern coordinates in the sources I found, so every line on the map is approximate. Switch them off with the "East/West Jersey lines" box above the map. Confidence is given for each.

        In short: the red line is the proposed 1688 Coxe–Barclay line between East and West Jersey. The East Jersey proprietors disowned it in 1695 and it was rescinded in 1718 (one source says 1719), so it was never a permanent proprietary boundary, although parts of it survive as county boundaries. The dotted line is the Lawrence line of 1743, the one finally accepted as the division.

        Coxe–Barclay line
        1688        		From the end of the Keith line: down the South Branch of the Raritan, up the North Branch, up the Lamington (Black) River, a straight line to the head of the Passaic, then the Pompton and Pequannock Rivers up to 41°N, then due east to the Hudson. Some sources instead carry it up the Pequannock to its head and then northeast along today's eastern Sussex boundary. It survives as the eastern boundaries of Morris and Sussex counties and the northern boundary of Somerset. The East Jersey proprietors disowned it in 1695 and it was rescinded in 1718 (one source says 1719). Probable course; the waypoints are from the sources and the river lines are this map's schematic ones.Solid line: Pompton and Pequannock Rivers up to 41°N, then due east. Dashed line: the Passaic from its head to the Pompton, which the sources imply but do not state. Faint dotted line: the alternative reading up the Pequannock. The stretch from the Lamington River to the head of the Passaic is off the map to the southwest.
        Lawrence line
        1743        		John Lawrence's survey of the 1676 straight line from the Delaware to the south end of Little Egg Harbor. The NJ Supreme Court adopted it in 1855, and Munsell says it was "finally settled upon as the true one" and runs through Budd's Lake. NJ Geological Survey Bulletin 67 says it had no effect on county boundaries. Proposed position: I drew a straight line from Budd's Lake (Munsell) toward Little Egg Harbor. Its true position could differ by a few miles, and Munsell's other remark that it touched only the Wallkill headwaters does not fit it well.Dashed line along the western edge. East of it is East Jersey.
        Thornton line c. 1696
        Mahacomack line 1775        		Named in the sources, but I found no waypoints for either. The Mahacomack line was proposed by West Jersey and never adopted.Not drawn.
        • On the 1688 course, everything on this map north and west of the Passaic, Pompton and Pequannock Rivers fell on the West Jersey side (the line gave roughly present Morris and Sussex counties to West Jersey). On the 1743 Lawrence line, which was the one finally accepted, all of it is East Jersey. Until after the Revolution the West Jersey proprietors still claimed the Coxe–Barclay line and the East Jersey proprietors the Keith line extended (Munsell).
        • The shading and the orange township names show the West Jersey side on the 1688 course only. On the 1743 Lawrence line none of it would be West Jersey. The stretch from the head of the Passaic to the Lamington River is off the map, so Chester and Mendham are shaded West Jersey on the assumption that it lies south of them. That is an assumption, supported only by those two townships being in Morris and not Somerset County.
        • The 1707 act setting Essex's bounds ran "the said division line to Pequaneck River, where it meets Passaick River". Munsell says this put part of Morris County in Essex and part in Burlington, and that the line meant was Coxe–Barclay.
        • Unresolved: Munsell also says the Coxe–Barclay line "passed about five miles north of Morristown". That does not fit the Passaic–Pompton–Pequannock waypoints above, so I did not draw it. Whether a particular Kent tract was granted by East Jersey or West Jersey proprietors is a question for its chain of title. I have not tested that for any Kent tract.
        • To pin the lines down, the surveyor's papers at the New Jersey Historical Society (Guide to the John Lawrence Surveyor Papers, MG 258) are the next place to look.

        What was checked and corrected when the two maps were combined
        • Beaver Brook and Burnt Meadow. The Essex map still showed them near Pompton Plains. They are now where the Morris map had them: Helmer Kent's Beaver Brook plantation (D) between Rockaway and Denville, and Hendrick Kent's house (A) at the head of Burnt Meadow Brook below Green Pond. The eight Morris deeds from the deed workbook now appear under their lettered Morris entries instead of as separate numbered markers.
        • Morris Turnpike (1801). It ran Chatham, Madison, Morristown, Walnut Grove, Succasunna, Drakesville, Stanhope. The road through Mendham and Chester that the Morris map called the Morris Turnpike was the Washington Turnpike (1806).
        • Newark & Mount Pleasant Turnpike (1806). It entered Morris at Cook's Bridge and ran by Whippany and Littleton to join the Union Turnpike near Dover. It did not pass through Denville and Rockaway. That road was the Parsippany & Rockaway Turnpike (1809): Pine Brook, Troy, Parsippany, Denville, Rockaway, Mount Pleasant. It is now drawn.
        • Newark & Pompton Turnpike (1806). The main line ran Bloomfield, Montclair, Verona, Cedar Grove, Singac, Pompton Plains. Bloomfield Avenue through Caldwell to the Pine Brook toll gate was its branch.
        • Paterson & Hamburg Turnpike (1806). Now drawn in full from Acquackanonk Landing through Paterson and Pompton, up the Pequannock valley to Newfoundland.
        • Union Turnpike (1804). Morristown, Dover, Mount Pleasant, Berkshire Valley, toward Sparta. Its line between Morristown and Dover is a general route only.
        • First and Second Mountain were drawn too far east. They, and the township lines that follow them (Caldwell–Bloomfield, Orange–Livingston), have moved west. The Second Mountain marker moved onto the ridge.
        • Markers moved to the correct side of a river or line: Manchester (north bank of the Passaic, opposite Paterson), the east-bank Saddle River Township lot, the Acquackanonk woodlots, the Long Hill lot (M), and Isaac Kent's Passaic-bank lot (E, Morris side).
        • Franklin Forge lot (G) moved north to Den Brook at Franklin, in present Denville. The 1791 deed runs "towards Denbrook" next to Isaac Canfield, and an 1816 list has Isaac Canfield's forge at Franklin.
        • The "Kent place" (C) moved to the lower (outlet) end of Green Pond, which is drawn in its real northeast–southwest position.
        • Rivers. The Passaic's loop around Paterson, its course from Two Bridges to Chatham, the Rockaway, Whippany, Pompton and Pequannock rivers and Beaver, Den, Green Pond and Burnt Meadow brooks were redrawn to one consistent course.
        • Morris township lines (c. 1830) were redrawn: the Rockaway River as the Pequannock–Hanover line, Jefferson west of Green Pond, and Randolph, Roxbury, Morris, Chatham, Mendham and Chester. They are approximate and not from a survey. Rockaway Township was not formed until 1844.
